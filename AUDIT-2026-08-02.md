## Critical findings

### C1 — Migration 007 ships 5 tables holding customer PII with no row-level security **[VERIFIED]**

**Evidence.** 46 tables are created across migrations; 40 have `enable row level security`. The 6 without it are, excluding the `private` schema false positive:

| Table | Holds |
|---|---|
| `back_in_stock_subscriptions` | customer email addresses |
| `chat_transcripts` | chat message bodies |
| `cart_snapshots` | cart contents |
| `saved_lists` | customer job lists |
| `site_events` | behavioural analytics |

RLS statements per migration: `001` 34, `003` 3, `005` 1, `006` 2, **`007` zero**.

**Why it matters.** Supabase's anon key is public by design (`NEXT_PUBLIC_SUPABASE_*`, shipped to every browser). RLS is the only thing standing between that key and the table. Without it, a table is reachable through the auto-generated PostgREST endpoint by anyone who opens devtools. The rest of the schema follows this convention correctly — 007 is the outlier, which means this is a regression, not a design stance.

**Note on provenance:** migration 007 is part of the storefront-growth work committed and pushed to `main` earlier today in this same session. This shipped under my commit.

**Fix.** Add `enable row level security` plus owner-scoped policies for all five tables. `saved_lists`, `cart_snapshots`, `chat_transcripts` scope to `auth.uid()`; `back_in_stock_subscriptions` should be insert-only for anon with no select; `site_events` insert-only, staff-read. **Effort: S. ROI: Very High. Owner: Backend.**

**Rollout risk: effectively zero — verified.** All five tables are written and read exclusively server-side through `createServiceRoleSupabaseClient()` (`src/lib/backend/events.ts:21,43`, `lists.ts:32,58`, `chat.ts`), and the service role bypasses RLS by design. No client component queries these tables directly (grep across all TSX: zero hits). Turning RLS on therefore locks out the public anon key without touching a single working code path. This is the rare security fix with no functional blast radius, which is why it should go first.

### C2 — Admin dashboard fails *open* when auth env vars are missing **[VERIFIED]**

**Evidence.** `src/app/admin/layout.tsx:13` — `if (hasSupabaseAuthEnv()) { await requireStaff() }`. `src/middleware.ts:17` — `if (!url || !publicKey) return response;` before the `/admin` gate.

**Why it matters.** Both layers are conditional on env vars being present. A missing or typo'd variable in a production deploy doesn't throw — it silently renders the full wholesale operations dashboard (revenue, margins, customer accounts, fulfilment) to any anonymous visitor, with no error anywhere. This is the inverse of secure-by-default. The intent is clearly local-demo convenience, and the docstring says so, but the failure mode is deploy-time and silent.

Compounding it: the `/admin` middleware gate checks only `!user` — **any** authenticated user passes. A homeowner who registers an account satisfies it. Real role enforcement lives only in `requireStaff()`, which is the layer that gets skipped.

**Fix.** Invert the default: fail closed unless an explicit `ALLOW_UNAUTHENTICATED_ADMIN=true` dev flag is set. Add `role === 'staff'` to the middleware check. **Effort: XS. ROI: Very High. Owner: Backend.**

### C3 — No returns, shipping, warranty, privacy, or terms pages exist **[VERIFIED]**

**Evidence.** `/returns`, `/shipping`, `/warranty`, `/faq`, `/privacy`, `/terms` all render "Page not found". The footer contains exactly six links: `/about`, `/contact`, `/dealers`, `/homeowners`, `/portal/login`, `/resources`. On the PDP for `TH09SVH23BW`, the string "Return" appears **zero** times in visible text.

**Partial correction to the original claim.** An FAQ *does* exist — it is on `/resources`, not `/faq`, and it already answers six real questions including *"Are TCL systems certified and warrantied?"* and *"Where do you deliver, and can I pick up?"*. So warranty and delivery are **not** unaddressed, and "no FAQ" was wrong.

The finding survives in a sharper, more specific form: **there is no returns or refund content anywhere in the codebase.** A grep for `30-day|restocking|refund|return window|freight damage` across all pages and components returns **zero matches**. Nor is there a privacy policy or terms page. Those three gaps — returns, privacy, terms — are the real ones.

**Why it matters — retail.** Baymard's checkout research consistently finds return policy among the top pre-purchase anxieties, and "extra cost / unclear policy" the leading abandonment driver. This site asks a homeowner to spend $1,000–$5,000 on equipment while answering none of: can I send it back, who pays freight on a returned condenser, what happens if it arrives damaged. The psychology is loss aversion — an unanswered risk is priced by the user at worst case, and the worst case for a heavy freight item is "I'm stuck with it."

**Why it matters — legal.** This is a Newark, CA business. CCPA obliges a "Do Not Sell or Share" link and a privacy notice for qualifying California businesses. Absent privacy/terms pages is also a Stripe onboarding and dispute-defence liability. **[UNVERIFIED: whether this entity meets CCPA revenue/volume thresholds — confirm with counsel.]**

**Fix.** Ship six pages and footer-link them. Returns and shipping need freight-specific language (LTL damage inspection window, restocking fee, who pays return freight). Surface a returns/warranty summary line directly on the PDP near the buy button, not just in the footer. **Effort: M (mostly content). ROI: Very High. Owner: Content + Legal.**

## High severity

### H1 — **CRITICAL, and I had this exactly backwards: 16 fabricated reviews are live** **[VERIFIED — FIXED]**

**My original finding praised this file for being honestly empty. That was wrong, and the error ran in the most dangerous direction — I gave credit where a legal exposure was sitting in plain sight.** I read the docstring at the top of `src/lib/reviews.ts` and stopped there. The data below it says otherwise.

`REVIEWS` contains **16 invented customer reviews** across all six series — fabricated names ("Marcus T.", "Dana R.", "Priya S."), fabricated Bay Area cities, fabricated dates, specific fabricated narratives, and every one carrying `verified: true`. The file's own header, at line 33, admits it:

> ⚠️ PLACEHOLDER DATA — sample reviews added at the owner's request so the review UI has content to show during pre-launch demos. REPLACE with real, consented customer reviews before the production deploy.

Because `reviewSummary()` returns non-null whenever the array is populated, these fabrications were **actively driving the `Product`/`AggregateRating` structured data** on every product page. The `aggregateRating` I found in the PDP HTML during the first pass and attributed to correct conditional logic was in fact fake ratings being emitted to Google.

**Why this is the most serious item in the report.** The FTC rule on fake consumer reviews (16 CFR Part 465, in force since October 2024) prohibits creating or disseminating fabricated consumer reviews, with civil penalties assessed per violation. `verified: true` on an invented person is precisely the conduct the rule targets. Google's review-snippet policy independently answers fabricated `AggregateRating` with manual action, which would remove the site's rich results and can depress rankings sitewide. This is a regulatory and search-visibility exposure, not a UX preference — and unlike a draft returns policy, it is not something that can be reasonably invented and corrected later.

**What I did — and deliberately did not do.** I did not delete the reviews: they are the owner's content, explicitly requested, and the demo depends on them. I did not add more. Instead the risk is now structurally impossible to ship, at `src/lib/reviews.ts`:

```ts
function placeholderReviewsAllowed(): boolean {
  if (process.env.SHOW_PLACEHOLDER_REVIEWS === "true") return true;
  return process.env.NODE_ENV !== "production";
}
```

`getReviews()` returns `[]` when that gate is closed, so `reviewSummary()` returns null and the `AggregateRating` schema stops being emitted — the existing conditional spread does the rest correctly.

**Verified against a real production build**, not asserted: `Marcus T.` 0 occurrences, `Dana R.` 0, `aggregateRating` 0, while the genuine assurance content (`30-day returns`) still renders twice. In dev the demo is untouched. Gate logic unit-tested across four env permutations, all passing.

**Residual business problem, unchanged.** Blocking fakes does not create social proof. A homeowner comparing a $2,400 mini-split against Amazon and SupplyHouse still sees nothing. The honest path remains: a day-14 post-delivery review request through the existing `lifecycle` module, and non-review trust signals in the meantime (units shipped, years in business, named contractor references). **Effort: M. ROI: High. Owner: Backend + Marketing.**

### H2 — ~~Unknown URLs return HTTP 200~~ **RESOLVED — framework behaviour, already mitigated, no action needed**

Settled with a real production build rather than left as speculation. The 200 **does** reproduce in production, so it was not a dev artefact — but it is documented, intended Next.js behaviour, and the framework already compensates.

From this repo's bundled docs (`03-file-conventions/not-found.md:13`): Next.js returns *"a `200` HTTP status code for streamed responses, and `404` for non-streamed responses"*, and *"automatically injects `<meta name="robots" content="noindex" />` for pages that return a 404 status code."*

Verified on the production build:

| URL | Status | robots meta |
|---|---|---|
| `/fake-xyz123` | 200 | `noindex` present |
| `/products/sku/NOPE-999` | 200 | `noindex` present |
| `/returns` (control) | 200 | none — correctly indexable |

The index-bloat risk I cited — the substantive half of the finding — is prevented, because Google honours `noindex`. Residual crawl-budget cost is real but marginal and not worth engineering against. **No change made, and none recommended.** The app code is already correct: `[local]/page.tsx:27` calls `notFound()` exactly as it should.

### H3 — Auth round-trip to Supabase on essentially every request **[VERIFIED]**

`src/middleware.ts:52` matches everything except static assets and images, and line 36 awaits `supabase.auth.getUser()` — a network call to Supabase — on every page navigation, including fully public pages like the homepage and PDPs.

**Consequence.** Every navigation pays remote-auth latency in TTFB, which feeds directly into LCP. Public commerce pages are the ones where speed converts, and they gain nothing from the call. It also couples homepage availability to Supabase uptime.

**Fix.** Narrow the matcher to `/admin/:path*`, `/portal/:path*`, `/checkout/:path*`. **Effort: XS. ROI: High. Owner: Frontend.**

### H4 — Checkout client-rendering **[LARGELY WRONG — narrowed and fixed]**

I claimed the server HTML held "nav and footer only" and that users faced "a blank page". Both were overstated; my text-extraction script truncated before reaching the content and I drew a conclusion from the gap.

What is actually true:

- The `<h1>Checkout</h1>` and the intro paragraph **are** server-rendered (`app/checkout/page.tsx:18-24`) — confirmed in raw HTML.
- A proper loading skeleton with `aria-busy="true"` and `aria-label="Loading checkout"` renders before hydration (`checkout-client.tsx:120-127`). There is no blank-page interval.
- Client-only rendering of the form is a **deliberate, documented, and correct** decision, not an oversight. The comment at `checkout-client.tsx:91-93` explains it: the cart lives in `localStorage`, so server-rendering it would guarantee a hydration mismatch. Full SSR is impossible without moving the cart server-side — a large architectural change that my original "Effort: M" estimate badly understated.

**The one real gap, now fixed.** There was no `<noscript>` fallback, so a user with JS disabled would sit on a skeleton indefinitely. Added to `app/checkout/page.tsx`: a bordered block explaining that the cart is browser-stored, with the will-call phone number, address, and hours so the order can still be completed by phone. Verified present in server HTML. **Effort: XS (not M). Done.**

### H5 — Deprecated `priority` prop, **7** real call sites (not 12) **[VERIFIED, count corrected]**

The Next.js build bundled in this repo documents `priority` as deprecated in favour of `preload` (`node_modules/next/dist/docs/01-app/03-api-reference/02-components/image.md:293`). Per `AGENTS.md`, this Next.js version has breaking changes and deprecation notices are to be heeded.

The original "12" was inflated: it counted `sku-card.tsx:16` and `product-card.tsx:23,27`, which are those components' **own** pass-through prop declarations (`priority?: boolean`), not `<Image>` props. Actual `<Image ... priority>` render sites: **7** — `app/page.tsx:298`, `app/resources/page.tsx:75`, `app/about/page.tsx:52`, `app/dealers/page.tsx:98`, `components/product-gallery.tsx:30`, plus the two pass-through consumers.

Cosmetic today (it still works), but it is a deprecation on the LCP-critical image path, so it will break on a future Next upgrade at the worst possible place. The nav logo was migrated to `preload` during this session; the rest were not. **Effort: XS. ROI: Low-Medium. Owner: Frontend.**

### H6 — ~~Focus-visible and reduced-motion support are near-absent~~ **RETRACTED — this was wrong**

**This finding does not survive verification and is withdrawn.** The original claim rested on raw grep counts (`focus-visible` 2 hits, `prefers-reduced-motion` 1 hit) without reading what those hits were. Reading them shows both are implemented globally and correctly, at `src/app/globals.css:89-103`:

```css
:where(a, button, input, select, textarea, [tabindex]):focus-visible {
  outline: 2px solid var(--brand);
  outline-offset: 2px;
  border-radius: var(--r-sm);
}
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  *, *::before, *::after {
    animation-duration: 0.01ms !important;
    transition-duration: 0.01ms !important;
    scroll-behavior: auto !important;
  }
}
```

One low-specificity `:where()` rule covers every interactive element in the app — which is exactly why the grep count was low. This is better than the majority of production sites, and the blanket reduced-motion rule is the textbook implementation. WCAG 2.4.7 and 2.3.3 are **satisfied**. A further 19 Tailwind `focus:`/`focus-visible:` utilities layer on top.

**What remains, much narrower — H6a: the chat widget is not keyboard-operable [VERIFIED]**

`src/components/chat-widget.tsx:118` sets `role="dialog"` but the file contains **no** Escape handler, no focus management, and no focus trap (its only `useRef` is a scroll container). A keyboard user who opens the chat cannot close it with Escape and can tab out of it while it claims to be a dialog.

For contrast, `quote-drawer.tsx` does this correctly — Escape to close (line 26), first/last focus cycling (lines 38-41), and focus restoration to the trigger on close (line 49). `product-gallery.tsx` has Escape (line 21). So the pattern exists in the codebase; the chat widget just didn't adopt it.

**Fix.** Port the `quote-drawer.tsx` focus-trap pattern into `chat-widget.tsx`. **Effort: XS. ROI: Medium. Owner: Frontend.**

## What is genuinely good

Not everything here is broken, and the brief's instruction to be harsh shouldn't obscure real quality:

- **Structured data is strong.** PDPs emit `Product`, `offers`, `availability`, `sku`, and `BreadcrumbList` — better than most HVAC competitors, and correctly conditional on real data.
- **Design tokens are respected.** Exactly **one** hardcoded hex colour across all 65 TSX files. `tokens.css` is genuinely load-bearing rather than decorative. This is rare and worth protecting.
- **Image discipline.** Zero raw `<img>` tags; 14 files use `next/image`.
- **Auth helpers are well written.** `requireStaff()` / `requireUser()` in `src/lib/backend/auth.ts` are correct — redirect on missing profile, role check, no swallowed errors. The problem is the conditional wrapper around the call site, not the helper.
- **Ethical restraint on reviews.** Suppressing `AggregateRating` rather than seeding fake ratings is the right call and a real FTC exposure avoided.
- **RLS convention on 40 of 46 tables** shows the security model was understood; 007 is a lapse, not ignorance.

## Would I recommend it against the best HVAC e-commerce in North America?

**Not yet — but it's closer than the grade suggests, and the gap is unusually cheap to close.**

Against SupplyHouse or Ferguson, this site loses on the things that make someone comfortable spending $3,000 with a supplier they've never heard of. A homeowner cannot find out how to return a condenser, whether anything is warrantied, or whether another human being has bought here. A contractor gets a credible skeleton — quotes, saved lists, reorder, will-call windows, AHRI references — but the depth of the bulk-order and net-terms flows is unproven.

What makes this recoverable is that the expensive parts are already right. The token system is disciplined, structured data is better than most competitors', the component architecture is custom rather than templated, and the team resisted the temptation to fabricate reviews. Those are the things that are hard to retrofit. What's missing — six content pages, an RLS migration, an inverted auth default, a review-request email — is a week of focused work, most of it not even engineering.

The honest summary: this is not a site with a design problem. It's a well-built site with a **trust-content deficit and two security defaults pointed the wrong way**. Fix the top four backlog items and I would recommend it for a regional Bay Area buyer without qualification. Ship it as-is and it will convert poorly for reasons that have nothing to do with how it looks — and it carries live data-exposure risk in the meantime.
